This is the practical version of how the Dionisia Millanar record got found — not a polished how-to, just what actually works when you're searching Bohol and Caraga region records with no head start.

Start with what exists, not what you're looking for

Two completely separate record systems cover the Philippines, and they don't overlap cleanly: church records and civil registration. Knowing which one to check first depends entirely on the decade you're searching.

Catholic church records — baptisms, marriages, burials — were kept consistently from 1569 onward, under directives from the Council of Trent. Civil registration, by contrast, wasn't required by law until 1930, and wasn't centrally overseen until the Bureau of Census and Statistics was created in 1932. For anything before the 1930s, church records are usually your only real option — and for anything before 1889, they're the only option, full stop.

The record collections that actually matter here

On FamilySearch (free to search, free account required to view most images), the relevant collections for Bohol and the wider Caraga/Central Visayas area are:

  • Philippines, Central Visayas, Civil Registration — covers Bohol, Cebu, Negros Oriental, and Siquijor specifically
  • Philippines Births and Baptisms, 1642–1994
  • Philippines Marriages, 1723–1957
  • Philippines Deaths and Burials, 1726–1957
  • Philippines, Catholic Church Records, 1520–2014
  • Philippines Civil Registration (Spanish Period), 1706–1911
  • Philippines Civil Registration (Local), 1888–1986
  • Philippines Civil Registration (Archives Division), 1902–1945
  • Philippines Civil Registration (National), 1945–1984

Search by name on the collection page rather than browsing blind — and when you get a hit, don't stop there. Cross-check it against the equivalent church record for the same event if one exists; the two often carry slightly different details, and the mismatch can be a clue in itself.

The gap almost everyone hits

A lot of civil records from the first half of the twentieth century were destroyed during the Second World War. If a search comes back empty for the 1940s specifically, that's not necessarily a dead end — it may just mean the record didn't survive the war, and church records or oral family history are your only remaining path for that window.

It's also worth knowing that registration itself was never complete. Distance from a civil registrar, inconsistent local government, and plain non-compliance meant many births, marriages, and deaths across the Philippines were simply never recorded anywhere at all — which is exactly why a family with "no prior record-keeping," like this one, is the rule in this region, not the exception.

Finding the right parish

Civil and church jurisdictions in the Philippines usually follow the same boundaries, so once you know the municipality, you generally know which parish or diocese to search. The Catholic Directory of the Philippines lists every archdiocese, diocese, and parish alongside its founding date — useful for confirming a parish actually existed yet when your ancestor would have been baptized there.

A Bohol-specific note

Bohol's own colonial history is worth knowing if you're researching further back: the island saw two major uprisings against Spanish rule — the Tamblot Uprising in 1621 and the Dagohoy Rebellion, which ran from 1744 to 1829 and stands as the longest sustained revolt in Philippine history. American forces took control of the province on 17 March 1900. None of that shows up in a birth record, but it's useful context for understanding why record-keeping in certain periods and areas is thinner than others.

When the trail actually goes cold

Sometimes it just does — for now. Log what you searched and what came back empty, not just what you found; a documented gap saves you from repeating the same dead-end search six months later, and tells the next person (family or otherwise) exactly where to pick up.
