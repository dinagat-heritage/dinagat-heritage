Island & Culture

Older than
the province.

The name itself comes from the Visayan root dagat — sea. Dinagat means, roughly, "of the sea" or "sea-like" — which tells you almost everything about how life here has always worked, long before it had a provincial capital or a seat in Congress.

The first people

The Mamanwa were here first, by a margin measured in tens of thousands of years, not centuries. They're a Negrito ethnic group, among the earliest inhabitants of the wider Caraga region, and — unlike almost everyone who arrived after them — they were never really conquered so much as quietly outlasted. Traditionally hunter-gatherers with deep animist traditions centered on forest and nature spirits, the Mamanwa still number only a few thousand people today, and their communities in Dinagat continue to hold a recognized place as one of the province's indigenous peoples under Philippine law.

Later Austronesian settlers — speakers of Cebuano and Surigaonon — arrived and blended with existing Mamanwa communities rather than displacing them outright, forming the mixed Visayan-speaking population that makes up most of the province today. Before Spain ever arrived, this stretch of coast sat within the trading reach of the Rajahnate of Butuan, a pre-colonial polity on nearby Mindanao known for its maritime trade networks.

A colonial afterthought

Spain never fully bothered with Dinagat the way it did larger, more strategically useful islands. A Spanish town was established at Surigao in 1609, and Dinagat was folded into its jurisdiction on paper — but its dense terrain and the Mamanwa's resistance to assimilation kept much of the island only loosely governed for most of the colonial era. The municipality of Dinagat itself wasn't formally founded until 1855, making it the oldest town in what would eventually become its own province — a century and a half before that province existed.

Becoming a province — twice

This is the part of Dinagat's history that actually made national news, and it's a genuinely strange story. In 2006, Republic Act No. 9355 split Dinagat off from Surigao del Norte to form its own province. A plebiscite that December passed narrowly — roughly 70,000 votes in favor against 63,000 against — and Dinagat Islands became the Philippines' newest province, with San Jose as its capital.

Then it got complicated. Opponents challenged the law in court, arguing Dinagat didn't meet the minimum land area or population thresholds the Local Government Code requires for provincehood. In February 2010, the Supreme Court agreed — and voided the province entirely, along with every official elected to run it. For over a year, Dinagat Islands legally didn't exist as a province.

Then, in April 2011, the same Supreme Court reversed itself, ruling 9-6 that island provinces are exempt from the land-area rule under the Local Government Code, and reinstated the province and its officials. Critics called it a flip-flop; the court held firm through further appeals, and by September 2012 the ruling was declared final. Since then, Dinagat Islands has stayed a province — but for a while, its status was genuinely up in the air in a way most provinces never have to think about.

What the island runs on now

Today Dinagat sits inside what's sometimes called the Philippines' nickel belt — the province hosts around ten active nickel mining operations, part of a wider Caraga region that supplies a significant share of the country's metallic mineral output. Nickel is a genuinely important export, tied into global demand for electric-vehicle batteries and clean-energy technology.

It's also a source of real tension. Reporting through 2025 documented residents in mining towns like Tubajon, Libjo, and Cagdianao raising concerns about water pollution, declining fish catches, and health complaints tied to nearby operations — while mining companies point to local jobs and community development programs they fund. Both things are true at once, and it's an ongoing story rather than a settled one — worth knowing if you're picturing the island as postcard-only.

Fiestas, not just history

Alongside all of that, ordinary cultural life carries on the way it does across the Visayas — bayanihan (communities pitching in to help each other, most visibly when literally moving a house), rondalla string-band music, and tinikling among the folk dances still performed at local celebrations. The province's own signature event, Bugal sa Dinagat, is built specifically around celebrating the island's culture, arts, and traditions — a fairly recent addition to the calendar for a fairly recent province.

The Other Shore: Cortes, Bohol

Dinagat is only half the family's geography. The paternal side traces to Cortes, a small coastal town on Bohol — the birthplace of Dionisia Millanar, the earliest confirmed record in this archive. Two islands, one family — so Cortes earns its own place here too.

Cortes, Bohol — a few facts

  • The name has two competing origin stories. The provincial government's own account says two older barangays, Pamingwitan and Dayhangan, were merged into one town during the building of the local church and given the name "Cortes" — from a Spanish word meaning roughly "most courteous." Other sources trace it instead to the Spanish conquistador Hernando Cortés. Bohol's own records favor the first story.
  • It sits barely 10 kilometers from Tagbilaran — Bohol's capital — about a fifteen-minute drive today. Dionisia would have lived her entire life within easy reach of the provincial center, without necessarily needing to go there often.
  • A coastal, fishing-based community from the start — one of its original barangays, Malabago, was renamed Pamingwitan specifically because of the way the people there fished.
  • Small and staying that way. Cortes remains a fifth-class municipality today, with a population still under 19,000 — roughly the same modest scale of town it likely was in Dionisia's lifetime.
  • Self-governed before Spain ever arrived — Cortes had its own barangay leadership structure well before colonization, the same as most of the Visayas.

And Metro Manila: Where the Family Kept Moving

The Dinagat Association Manila photos already showed one generation making that move — Lancelot Jason and his siblings, born in Pasig, are proof it never really stopped. The same instinct that filled that 1960s ballroom with Dinagat Islanders kept pulling later generations toward Metro Manila too.

Pasig — a few facts

  • Older than most of the country's cities — the Augustinians established Pasig in 1572, making it one of the oldest continuously governed towns in the Philippines, decades before most of what's now Metro Manila existed as anything formal.
  • The name comes from a river, not a person. Linguist Jose Villa Panganiban traced "Pasig" to an old Sanskrit word for a river flowing from one body of water into another — describing the Pasig River itself, which connects Laguna de Bay to Manila Bay.
  • It was Rizal province's capital for most of the 20th century, right up until 1975, when Pasig was folded into the newly created Metro Manila instead.
  • Ruled by a queen before Spain arrived. Local tradition holds that around 1450, Pasig was governed by Dayang Kalangitan, wife of Gat Lontok — one of the few pre-colonial rulers in the region remembered by name.
  • A real flashpoint in the revolution — one of the first major battles of the Katipunan uprising against Spain, in August 1896, was fought right here.
