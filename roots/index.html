Dionisia Millanar
Cortes, Bohol (1907–1955)

The earliest confirmed record in this family's archive — and where the paper trail currently runs out.

Dionisia Millanar

1907 – 30 August 1955

  • Born: 1907, Cortes, Bohol, Philippines
  • Died: 30 August 1955, in her hometown, at age 48
  • Line: Paternal — father's side of the family

Source: FamilySearch civil record. Verified 2026.

Nobody in the family kept a written record before this. No family bible, no bundle of letters, no photographs with names pencilled on the back — just the fact of a life lived in Cortes, on the island of Bohol, at the turn of the twentieth century, and the two dates that mark its edges.

The place — Cortes, Bohol

  • Location: a coastal municipality about 10km northeast of Tagbilaran City, roughly a 15-minute drive today
  • Before Dionisia's time: the area already had its own form of local governance before the Spanish arrived — the barangays of Malabago (later renamed Pamingwitan) and Dayhangan were prominent long before the town existed under its current name
  • The name itself: accounts differ — the provincial government's own history says "Cortes" comes from the Spanish word for "most courteous," adopted when two barangays merged during a church's construction; other records trace it instead to a Spanish governor-general named Cortés. Both versions are still told locally.
  • Character: traditionally a fishing community, still known today for its coastline and undeveloped beaches rather than tourist development
  • Scale: a modest municipality — around 18,000–19,000 people across 14 barangays, one of Bohol's smaller towns by income classification

What her lifetime actually spanned

Born in 1907, she would have grown up under early American colonial administration, following the end of nearly 350 years of Spanish rule just a few years earlier. She lived through the entire Japanese occupation of the Philippines during the Second World War — she would have been in her mid-thirties when it began — and died a decade after the war ended, in 1955, at 48 years old, in the same town where she was born.

That's not an unusual life for the place and period. It's exactly the kind of life that normally leaves no trace at all — which is what makes finding even this much worth writing down properly.

What's still missing

No record of her parents yet — that's a next step, not a dead end, since Bohol's civil registries and church records go back further than this, they just haven't been searched yet.

No photograph either, and this one may simply never be found. Every relative asked so far has come up empty — in Dionisia's lifetime, a camera wasn't something most families owned, and a photograph, when one happened at all, was rare enough to be genuinely special. It wasn't a given the way it is now; it was an occasion. If one turns up in an old box somewhere, it would be one of the most valuable things this archive could hold.

The family tree, so far

Two branches, built from records and from what relatives remember. The paternal side now traces back one generation further than we first thought — through Emilio Millanar and Pascuala Capayas, and their eight children, one of whom was Froilan Millanar Sr. The maternal side traces through the eight Jason siblings of Dinagat Island — eight and eight, it turns out, on both sides. Dates will keep filling in as more of the family contributes — this is a living document, not a finished chart.

A note on dates: ancestors who have passed are shown with full birth dates where known. Living relatives are shown by birth year only, as a simple safeguard for their privacy.

Before Isidaria — A First Marriage

Justo Piodo Jason had a first wife before Isidaria — her name isn't known yet, but the three children from that marriage carried forward under a slightly different spelling: Jazon, not Jason. It's the same kind of surname split documented earlier on the paternal side of this family — a midwife recording births by hand, at home, with no formal documentation to check the spelling against. The kind of small typographical drift that was almost inevitable in that era, and happened at least twice in this family alone.

If the Jazon spelling looks unfamiliar to anyone reading this — that's exactly why. Same family, same blood, one clerical hand away from the name everyone else carries.

The Next Generation — Jason Grandchildren

Every one of Justo and Isidaria's eight children went on to raise families of their own. This is the fullest count so far, branch by branch — seven families, documented with the same care as everything above.

A Reputation, Earned on the Drill Field

Panfilo wasn't just an association officer later in life — in his university days at Far Eastern University, he held a high rank in the school's ROTC corps. One morning, family legend has it, two classmates showed up late to drill, uniforms a mess. Panfilo walked the line in front of the whole formation, inspecting out loud as he went: "Unauthorized shirt." A tug at the sleeve. "Unauthorized boots." A glance down. Then he reached for one cadet's ungroomed moustache — "...and unauthorized face."

The corps lost it. The two latecomers, by all accounts, did not find it quite as funny, and went looking for him afterward. As the story's told in the family, it was the girls in his class who closed ranks around him before things went further — which is, as much as the joke itself, where "The Dashing Debonair" is said to have come from.

The Dinagat Association, Manila

Long before there was a website, there was a banner: "Dinagat Association Manila — Induction Ball — February 22." The exact year is still unknown, but the fashion in these photographs — the pompadours, the narrow ties, the barongs — places it somewhere in the late 1950s or 1960s. Whatever the date, one thing is certain: Dinagat Islanders living in Manila had already organized themselves into a proper association, complete with officers, an induction ceremony, a live band, and — going by the sheer number of empty glasses in these photographs — no shortage of good spirits either.

Panfilo Jason appears through several of these frames — by the family's own account, he served as one of the association's officers, which would explain why he turns up at the signing table more than once. By this point "The Dashing Debonair" was a title he'd already earned back at university — and looking at the tailoring on display here, it clearly followed him into adulthood.

Zacarias Millanar's Line

Zacarias was the eldest of Emilio and Pascuala's eight children. He married Leonora Santos, and they had four children — Emil, Zarah, Eric, and Edwin — whose own families make up the largest single branch documented on this page so far.

Edwin had seven children in total — Patrick, John, and Anna Marie with Rose David, and three more, Vincent, Wendel, and Tyron, with another woman whose name isn't recorded yet. Both families are equally part of this line; if anyone knows her name, that's exactly the kind of gap this archive is built to close.

The Ligason Line

Emilio Millanar's first marriage produced three more children — Porferia, Marcilina, and Jose — a full branch of the family in its own right, running parallel to Froilan's. Porferia Millanar married Porfirio Ligason, and their four children carried the Ligason name forward: Wilfredo, Cesar, Imelda, and Edgar.

Wilfredo Ligason married Imelda Maravilla in 1961, and they had three children — Weisner (b. 1994), Weidna (b. 1995), and Weimela (b. 2001). That's four generations from Emilio Millanar down to Weimela, documented with the same care as every other branch on this page.

Four generations, documented as thoroughly as any branch on this page — this is exactly the kind of thread the rest of the family, from either side, is invited to keep adding to.
